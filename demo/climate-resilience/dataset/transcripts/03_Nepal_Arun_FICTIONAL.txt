FICTIONAL TRAINING INTERVIEW — NOT REAL RESEARCH DATA
Participant alias: Arun
Setting: Fictional hillside settlement in central Nepal, Nepal
Interview language: English; scripted in English, not translated
All people, experiences, events and quotations below are invented.

Interviewer — Q1: Please tell me about your household and an ordinary day here.
Arun: I farm a small plot with my wife and take construction work when it is available. Our son attends the school across the valley, and my aunt lives in the next house. Most mornings begin with checking the animals and the water pipe. The path between households is part of our daily life. We visit, share tools and carry produce along it, so a damaged path changes more than the journey to market.

Interviewer — Q2: Describe a recent weather or environmental event that disrupted your life.
Arun: During the heavy rain imagined in this interview, stones fell onto the path and a section of the slope slipped below the road. We heard the noise before we could see what had happened. Nobody in our household was injured, but we stayed with relatives that night because the ground beside the house felt uncertain. After the rain stopped, I still watched every new crack. The end of the storm did not immediately end the worry.

Interviewer — Q3: How did the event affect drinking water, sanitation, electricity or access to care?
Arun: The pipe from the spring broke where the slope moved. We walked to another tap and carried water back, while people tried to repair the connection. The clinic was on the other side of the damaged road, so my aunt postponed a check-up. A water repair and a road repair seem like separate tasks, but both decide whether someone can manage an ordinary day. Services need routes and people, not just equipment.

Interviewer — Q4: What changed in your work, food supply, income or debts?
Arun: We could not send vegetables to market while the road was blocked. Some spoiled before a vehicle could come, and I missed construction work that would have paid for school materials. A trader offered a lower price because he had to arrange extra transport. I accepted it because the produce could not wait. Our loss was not visible as a broken building; it was the income that never arrived and the choices that became narrower.

Interviewer — Q5: How do you hear about danger, and how do you decide which information to trust?
Arun: I heard the rain warning on the radio and from a message shared by a local teacher. The forecast covered a large area, so we also checked the slope and stream ourselves. I trust information more when it explains what to watch for locally. People with poor phone reception need another route for the warning. A map on a screen is useful, but somebody still has to turn it into a decision about this particular path.

Interviewer — Q6: Tell me about deciding whether to leave or stay during an emergency.
Arun: We decided to leave when small stones kept falling and the path became slippery. My aunt did not want to go because she feared leaving the animals. We arranged for a neighbour to check them and carried her bag to a relative's house. The temporary place was crowded but felt safer for that night. Planning should include where people can stay for several days, not only telling them to leave at the first sign of danger.

Interviewer — Q7: Who helped, and what did neighbours or community groups do?
Arun: Several neighbours cleared a walking route before any machine arrived. Others shared food and let children sleep in their houses. I joined the work, but people who were injured or caring for relatives could not contribute in the same way. They should not receive less help because of that. Community work is valuable when contributions can differ, rather than when every household must supply the same labour to be considered deserving.

Interviewer — Q8: Did the disruption affect some people more than others?
Arun: My wife carried water and looked after our son while I helped reopen the path. I received thanks at the community meeting, while her work was described as normal household duty. I noticed that difference after she pointed it out. A family may appear to recover quickly because somebody is absorbing more work in private. We should ask each person what changed, instead of treating the household as if it had only one experience.

Interviewer — Q9: What support did you receive, and how much say did residents have in decisions?
Arun: An officer visited and photographed the damaged road. We were told that larger repairs would depend on a later budget decision. Residents asked about the smaller path to the water point, but it was not included in that first discussion. I understand that resources are limited. Still, the route used daily by people carrying water should count alongside the road used by vehicles. We need a chance to explain our priorities before the plan is settled.

Interviewer — Q10: What have you changed to prepare for the next disruption?
Arun: We redirect some runoff away from the house and check the pipe before the rainy season. A few families are testing small retaining measures with local advice. I do not know enough to say that every wall is safe, so I would like skilled inspection rather than copying a neighbour's design. There is a limit to what unpaid work can repair. Preparation should include maintenance and advice that people can actually afford.

Interviewer — Q11: Have you considered moving away, temporarily or permanently?
Arun: My brother works in the city and says I could join him. Seasonal work away from home might help with the repair bills, but someone would still need to farm and care for our aunt. Moving permanently would mean losing our plot and support nearby. I am considering options rather than making a final decision. It matters whether a move is a choice that expands our future or a last response after every local option has failed.

Interviewer — Q12: If residents could choose three changes, what would you prioritise?
Arun: I would choose a reliable path to water and school, timely inspection of unstable ground, and support for households whose income stops when roads close. Residents should help decide which routes are repaired first. I want our son to understand the risks without growing up afraid of every rainfall. We can contribute knowledge and labour, but technical advice and dependable public support are also necessary.
