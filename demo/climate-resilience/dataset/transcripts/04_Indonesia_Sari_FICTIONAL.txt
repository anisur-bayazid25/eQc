FICTIONAL TRAINING INTERVIEW — NOT REAL RESEARCH DATA
Participant alias: Sari
Setting: Fictional low-lying neighbourhood near Semarang, Indonesia
Interview language: English; scripted in English, not translated
All people, experiences, events and quotations below are invented.

Interviewer — Q1: Please tell me about your household and an ordinary day here.
Sari: I prepare breakfast food at home and sell it to workers on the morning shift. My husband does delivery jobs, and our nephew stays with us during school terms. We rent our house, so I cannot change every part of it. I value the customers who know my cooking and the neighbours who watch the stall when I step away. The neighbourhood is also a network that makes a small business possible.

Interviewer — Q2: Describe a recent weather or environmental event that disrupted your life.
Sari: In the flooding imagined for this interview, water entered the lane during the afternoon and rose again after heavy rain. I lifted the stove and food containers, but the doorway stayed wet for two days. Some neighbours said the water was routine, although this time it stayed longer. Calling a flood ordinary can hide the work it creates. We cleaned, waited, cleaned again, and tried to keep the business running between those tasks.

Interviewer — Q3: How did the event affect drinking water, sanitation, electricity or access to care?
Sari: We had to protect the drinking water containers from the dirty water in the lane. The electricity was interrupted, and I threw away some ingredients because the refrigerator could not keep them cool. Getting to the clinic meant using a longer route. My nephew helped carry containers, but he could not do that during school hours. Safe water was not only a question of supply; it was also storage, power and the route through the neighbourhood.

Interviewer — Q4: What changed in your work, food supply, income or debts?
Sari: Customers could not reach the stall and my husband had fewer delivery trips. I still owed rent, and I used the money set aside for supplies to pay it. Reopening meant buying ingredients before I had earned anything new. A neighbour lent me a small amount without interest, which helped. I want assistance to notice this gap between surviving the flood and being able to start earning again.

Interviewer — Q5: How do you hear about danger, and how do you decide which information to trust?
Sari: The neighbourhood group shared tide information and rain warnings on our phones. One neighbour printed the times and put them near the shop for people who were not in the group. I found that useful because messages disappear among many other conversations. We also needed to know when a route would become difficult. A warning becomes practical when it helps me decide when to move the stove and when to stop serving food.

Interviewer — Q6: Tell me about deciding whether to leave or stay during an emergency.
Sari: We did not leave immediately because the water was shallow inside and we feared losing the equipment. Later we stayed with a relative for one night. My landlord wanted to know whether we had damaged the floor, which added another worry. Tenants need a clear place in emergency planning. The person living in the house may be responsible for staying safe without having the authority or money to make the house safer.

Interviewer — Q7: Who helped, and what did neighbours or community groups do?
Sari: People in the lane lent raised shelves and shared a pump. We exchanged messages about open routes and checked on an older neighbour who lived alone. The support was quick because we knew one another. A delivery worker renting a room nearby was not in the phone group, so he learned about the route later. We added him after that. A neighbourhood network should be able to welcome people whose work keeps them away during meetings.

Interviewer — Q8: Did the disruption affect some people more than others?
Sari: I cleaned the cooking area before dawn, prepared less food, and then helped my nephew get to school by the longer route. My husband had his own work problems, but the cleaning and care tasks fell mostly to me. I was tired even on days when the stall did not open. People counted the lost sales, but nobody counted the extra hours needed to make the room usable again.

Interviewer — Q9: What support did you receive, and how much say did residents have in decisions?
Sari: Officials helped clear one drain, and that made a visible difference for part of the lane. I also asked about assistance for tenants, but I was told to speak to the property owner. The owner lived elsewhere and did not depend on the stall. I would like residents who rent to be heard directly. Support based only on ownership can overlook the people who are present during the flood and do the recovery work.

Interviewer — Q10: What have you changed to prepare for the next disruption?
Sari: I now store ingredients on higher shelves and keep a list of customers I can contact if the stall cannot open. Neighbours are trying a shared cleaning schedule for the drains. These changes help, but shelves do not stop water entering the lane. I cannot approve a major alteration to a rented house. We need coordination between residents, owners and public services, otherwise each person can do only a small part of the job.

Interviewer — Q11: Have you considered moving away, temporarily or permanently?
Sari: We have looked at rooms on higher ground, but the rent is higher and my customers would not follow me automatically. Staying keeps the business network, while moving could reduce some flooding. I do not feel there is a simple safe option. If help were available for restarting a small business elsewhere, moving would become more realistic. Advice to relocate should include a way to rebuild work and relationships, not just a new address.

Interviewer — Q12: If residents could choose three changes, what would you prioritise?
Sari: My priorities are drainage that is maintained, support that includes tenants, and small-business assistance that arrives before another rent payment is due. Meetings should include the people who work early mornings or late evenings. I want a neighbourhood where we prepare together but do not accept endless cleaning as normal. We should be able to earn, rest and send children to school without organising every ordinary day around the next flood.
