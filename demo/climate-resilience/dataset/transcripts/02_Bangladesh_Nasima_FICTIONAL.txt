FICTIONAL TRAINING INTERVIEW — NOT REAL RESEARCH DATA
Participant alias: Nasima
Setting: Fictional village in Patuakhali, Bangladesh
Interview language: English; scripted in English, not translated
All people, experiences, events and quotations below are invented.

Interviewer — Q1: Please tell me about your household and an ordinary day here.
Nasima: I sew clothes at home and grow vegetables when the soil allows it. My husband fishes, and my daughter helps her grandfather with his medicines after school. We do not have the same income every month, so we plan food and school costs carefully. I like living close to relatives. If a machine breaks or someone becomes ill, there is usually a person nearby who will come without being asked twice.

Interviewer — Q2: Describe a recent weather or environmental event that disrupted your life.
Nasima: In the event imagined for this interview, rain continued through the evening and water entered the yard before the strongest wind arrived. We put the sewing machine on a table and left for the shelter. When we returned, the floor was wet and salt marks remained on the vegetable beds. The house could be cleaned in a day, but the soil did not return to normal just because the water disappeared.

Interviewer — Q3: How did the event affect drinking water, sanitation, electricity or access to care?
Nasima: The nearby pond tasted salty after the flood, and the pump we usually used was surrounded by dirty water. I collected drinking water from a tank farther away. The queue was longer in the afternoon, so I went early and missed some sewing orders. Water was available somewhere, but getting it meant time, transport and somebody staying with my father. When people say the water problem is solved, I ask whose daily journey has become shorter.

Interviewer — Q4: What changed in your work, food supply, income or debts?
Nasima: My husband lost several fishing days and I could not use the machine until the room was dry. We bought vegetables that we normally grew and borrowed for school expenses. The lender knows us, which made borrowing easy, but the repayment still came before the next harvest. I accepted fewer orders because of the water trips. The flood connected our lost income to new household costs, rather than creating one separate bill.

Interviewer — Q5: How do you hear about danger, and how do you decide which information to trust?
Nasima: A volunteer announced the warning with a loudspeaker, and my daughter checked messages on her phone. I listened to both. The useful part was hearing when the shelter would open and which route was passable. Sometimes neighbours wait because earlier warnings did not lead to severe damage. I would rather receive an explanation of the uncertainty than a message that sounds completely certain and later seems wrong.

Interviewer — Q6: Tell me about deciding whether to leave or stay during an emergency.
Nasima: We left before dark because my father walks slowly. The shelter had space, but carrying his bedding and medicines was difficult. I worried about privacy and whether the toilets would be usable for him. A neighbour accompanied us, which helped. We were not choosing between a safe house and a dangerous shelter. We were choosing between different difficulties, and trying to make the least harmful choice with the information we had.

Interviewer — Q7: Who helped, and what did neighbours or community groups do?
Nasima: The women nearby shared cooking and took turns going for water. My niece watched the younger children while we cleaned the rooms. These arrangements were practical, not formal committees. One household had moved here recently and did not know whom to ask, so we invited them into the cooking group. Support becomes stronger when somebody notices who is missing, rather than assuming that every family already has relatives to rely on.

Interviewer — Q8: Did the disruption affect some people more than others?
Nasima: My daughter missed lessons while helping her grandfather, and I did unpaid care while trying to earn from sewing. My husband also worked hard, but his repair work was visible outside the house. Our extra work was inside. I want programmes to recognise both. If a household receives a water container, it still matters who fills it, who carries it, and who gives up paid work to make that possible.

Interviewer — Q9: What support did you receive, and how much say did residents have in decisions?
Nasima: We received food support, but the repair assistance required documents that had become wet. I visited the office twice and paid for transport both times. A staff member eventually helped me replace the papers. That kindness mattered, yet the process should not require finding a particularly helpful person. Residents should know the rules in advance and have a way to ask questions without losing several days of work.

Interviewer — Q10: What have you changed to prepare for the next disruption?
Nasima: We keep documents in a covered container and have changed part of the garden to crops we hope will tolerate the conditions better. I also keep the machine on a higher platform. These changes reduce some losses, but they cost money before we know whether they will work. We cannot raise the road ourselves. Household preparation and public maintenance need to support each other, or one blocked route can undo many careful preparations.

Interviewer — Q11: Have you considered moving away, temporarily or permanently?
Nasima: We have discussed renting a room near town for my daughter's studies, but moving the whole family would be much harder. My father wants to stay close to the people he knows. I do not want my daughter to feel she must give up education to look after everyone. A temporary move might help, although it would create two sets of expenses. For us, moving is a family negotiation rather than a single yes or no decision.

Interviewer — Q12: If residents could choose three changes, what would you prioritise?
Nasima: I would prioritise nearby drinking water, a shelter with toilets that older people can use, and assistance that does not disappear when documents are damaged. I would also ask officials to listen to the people who collect water and care for relatives. We can explain details that are missed in a quick visit. Fair support should reduce the work of recovery, not add another queue to an already crowded day.
