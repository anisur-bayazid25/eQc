FICTIONAL TRAINING INTERVIEW — NOT REAL RESEARCH DATA
Participant alias: Mere
Setting: Fictional coastal settlement on Viti Levu, Fiji
Interview language: English; scripted in English, not translated
All people, experiences, events and quotations below are invented.

Interviewer — Q1: Please tell me about your household and an ordinary day here.
Mere: I sell fish at the roadside and grow vegetables behind our house. My mother lives with us, and my two children attend the nearby school. In the afternoon I usually check on a neighbour before preparing food. This shore is where I learned to fish with my father. Our work, family visits and church activities fit together here, so an ordinary day is also a day of looking after people.

Interviewer — Q2: Describe a recent weather or environmental event that disrupted your life.
Mere: During the storm in this fictional interview, the wind began before sunset and the sea came over the road later that night. We moved the children upstairs at my cousin's house. In the morning our cooking shelter had lost its roof and the garden was covered with sand. The sea did not reach every house equally. Two houses farther inland stayed dry, which made the damage feel very uneven.

Interviewer — Q3: How did the event affect drinking water, sanitation, electricity or access to care?
Mere: Our rainwater tank survived, but dirty water entered the pipe connection. We boiled what we could while the power was out, and then shared water from a neighbour's covered tank. I made several trips carrying containers because my mother could not walk that distance. By the third day, collecting water took the hours I normally used to sell fish. A working tank matters, but so does being able to reach it.

Interviewer — Q4: What changed in your work, food supply, income or debts?
Mere: The boats stayed ashore and the roadside stalls were closed. I borrowed money to replace the cooking pots, and we ate the food we had saved for selling. When visitors returned, the catch was smaller and fuel cost more than I could manage. We did not lose only one week of income. We also lost the small reserve that usually helps us through the next difficult week.

Interviewer — Q5: How do you hear about danger, and how do you decide which information to trust?
Mere: The warning came by radio and then a youth volunteer walked from house to house. I trusted the volunteer because she explained when the road might flood and where to go. A message on a phone is useful only when the phone has charge and the words make sense. My mother heard the radio announcement but needed someone to explain the timing slowly. The same warning needed different ways of reaching people.

Interviewer — Q6: Tell me about deciding whether to leave or stay during an emergency.
Mere: The shelter was open, but I worried about leaving my mother's medicines and the fishing gear behind. The path was already wet when we decided to go. My cousin arrived with a cart, so we took the medicines, bedding and children together. We were fortunate to have that help. I would like evacuation planning to include people who cannot move quickly, rather than assuming everyone can carry a bag and walk.

Interviewer — Q7: Who helped, and what did neighbours or community groups do?
Mere: Neighbours cooked together beside the houses that still had roofs. One family shared water, another lent tools, and the youth group cleared fallen branches. That made us feel less alone. But the family renting a room near the landing place was not on the usual household list. They received food later than others. Knowing your neighbours helps, yet a support system should not depend entirely on being well known.

Interviewer — Q8: Did the disruption affect some people more than others?
Mere: I looked after my mother while repairing the stall, so my working day became longer even though my earnings became smaller. My brother helped with the roof, but he returned to his job after two days. People called us resilient, and I appreciated their kindness, but the extra care work stayed with me. Being able to continue does not mean the burden is light or shared equally.

Interviewer — Q9: What support did you receive, and how much say did residents have in decisions?
Mere: Emergency supplies arrived through a local committee. The first distribution was quick, but I did not know how the repair list was decided. Women who sold food asked to attend the planning meeting; it was held at the same time as the market. I do not think the organisers intended to exclude us. Still, a meeting is not open to everyone if attending means losing that day's income.

Interviewer — Q10: What have you changed to prepare for the next disruption?
Mere: We raised the tank connection and keep a sealed box for medicines and documents. The youth group practises checking on older residents. These are changes we can manage ourselves. Raising the whole house would cost much more than we earn. I also worry that a stronger wall at one property could send water towards another. Preparation should look at the whole neighbourhood, not just at the safest single house.

Interviewer — Q11: Have you considered moving away, temporarily or permanently?
Mere: My sister says we could move closer to town, where the road is higher. I understand the attraction, especially for school and medical visits. But rent would be a new expense, and I would lose the customers and relatives who help us here. Staying is not simply refusing advice. Moving is not simply becoming safe. We need to compare the risks and the support we would lose in either place.

Interviewer — Q12: If residents could choose three changes, what would you prioritise?
Mere: First, I would choose reliable drinking water that everyone can reach. Second, I would arrange transport for people who need help leaving. Third, I would hold planning meetings when working residents can attend. I want our children to have choices rather than inherit only our repairs. We know how to help each other, but we should not have to solve every large problem with a collection of small household savings.
